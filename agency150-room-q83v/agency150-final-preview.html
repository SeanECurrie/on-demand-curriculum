Executive Analysis

This is the analytical read in full, stated plainly. It is not a buy/pass call — that is a judgment for the investor with terms in hand. It is the evidence, worked through to the point where the decision actually turns.

What Agency150 is. A brand-new (launched September 2025), unfunded company whose product, Vega, uses AI to run psychologically-grounded interviews of an organization’s employees and customers at scale, sold as a boutique advisory engagement rather than software. Its public differentiation rests on two things that are real: a genuine behavioral-science credential (Chief Science Officer Lamar Pierce is the sitting Editor-in-Chief of Organization Science, a leading management journal T1) and an employee-experience / organizational focus that, as the competitive section shows, no funded competitor occupies.

The single most important framing point. The opportunity was described to the investor as AI that assesses CEO/executive candidates for hire. The public product does not do that — it conducts organizational and customer research. An AI-native executive-candidate-assessment company, in the form imagined, essentially does not exist (the genuine players in executive assessment are human-led, like ghSMART, or psychometric, like Hogan/SHL). This is not a reason to pass; it is the first thing to reconcile with the founders, because it changes what is being bought.

The investment logic, in one paragraph. The bull case is coherent: a real wave (AI compresses qualitative research 80–95% T2), a hard-to-fake science credential, and a defensible-looking niche (employee/org research) that the funded AI-research leaders have left alone while they crowd customer research. The bear case is heavier on today’s evidence: Vega is the least-proven entrant in its field — unfunded, with no named customers and no independent validation, while its rivals can name Microsoft, Amazon, and Nestlé and have raised $18M–$96M; the “human-advisory” differentiator is not unique (GetWhy claims it; ghSMART, with a 30-year, ~30,000-assessment dataset, out-classes it); and the core AI-interview engine is commoditizing into table stakes. The question the deal turns on is whether Vega’s EX-focus-plus-science-advisory bundle is a durable wedge or a temporary gap that a funded player or an incumbent (Qualtrics, Culture Amp) closes first.

◆ The read this analysis lands on

On the public evidence, this is a credible boutique-services business with a genuine niche and a real credential — not, yet, an obvious venture-scale bet, and not the company the investor was pitched. The factors that would change that read are not in the public record: whether the founders own defensible technology, whether real paying customers exist, what the terms are, and who actually introduced the deal. The analysis below is built so that when those answers arrive, the conclusion can move with them — in either direction. Where the evidence is thin, this document says so rather than dressing it up.

Every funding and market figure in this document was re-verified across two independent engines (professional web-data + independent search), with round amounts and cumulative totals labeled separately. Where a figure is an estimate, it is labeled an estimate with its arithmetic shown. Sources and tiers are in §10.

The Company · 01

Agency150 / Vega — Deep Profile

For a company this early, the team and the model are the investment — there is little operating history to weigh. This is the full read on what the company is, who is behind it, what is verified versus claimed, and where the real questions sit.

The entity, confirmed

Agency150 (domain agency150.com, brand “Agency 150,” product “Vega”) is a New York company, founded 2025 and launched publicly September 9, 2025, listed under “Research Services,” 11–50 employees, privately held. T1 The name is a search hazard — it collides with a Virginia veterinary code, generic “save 150+ hours” marketing copy, and unrelated companies whose products are also called “Vega” (a security-analytics firm, a wealth-management fintech, both of which have raised tens of millions). T2 None of those are this company; any “Vega raised $X” headline almost certainly refers to a different business. The identification here is high-confidence, anchored to the founder roster, the product, and an early notice from HR-industry analyst Josh Bersin.

The product and the model

Vega is described by the company as “the first AI-native advisory platform” company claim T5: an AI agent conducts conversational, “psychologically safe” interviews with an organization’s stakeholders — employees, customers, members — at scale, then decodes the transcripts into themes and “behavioural signals, readiness patterns, trust dynamics,” with a human team of behavioral scientists and ex-strategy-consultants interpreting the results. The pitch is a “best of three”: deeper than surveys (which give scores, not the “why”), faster than consultants (days, not months), and more rigorous than generic AI.

The model matters more than it first appears. Vega is sold as bespoke studies plus an annual subscription, supported by human advisors T1 — a services / advisory business, not a self-serve software business. Three consequences follow, and they shape the entire investment question: revenue is people-bound (consultants don’t scale like software seats), gross margins look like a consultancy’s rather than a SaaS company’s, and “traction” will show up as signed engagements and logos rather than sign-ups. The most valuable asset, on this model, is the people and the brand — not the software. That is the lens to read the team through.

⚑ The framing question, stated once, clearly

The opportunity reached the investor as AI that interviews and assesses CEO/executive candidates. An exhaustive search of the site, every 2026 LinkedIn post, founder commentary, and press found no executive-candidate-assessment or hiring product anywhere. T1 What is public is organizational and customer research (employee experience, customer insight, cultural diagnostics, M&A integration, “AI readiness”). The underlying engine is the same shape either way — AI conducting psychologically-safe interviews at scale — but the market is different. The closest adjacency is a “Due Diligence” use case (surveying a target’s workforce during M&A), which is adjacent in vocabulary to “assessing leadership” but different in function from assessing an individual CEO. This is the first thing to reconcile with the founders.

The Market · 02

Market Structure & Dynamics

The category is real, large, and recently legitimized at the boardroom level. But its structure is the heart of the bear case: the thing that makes Vega possible — cheap, capable AI — is also what makes it hard to defend, and Vega’s one piece of white space is also its most legally exposed.

The three markets Vega sits across

Vega plays at the intersection of three markets, none of which is precisely “its” market. The global insights industry is the outer boundary: ~$153B in 2024 (ESOMAR), splitting into ~$56B research services, ~$62B research software, and ~$35B reporting. T1 The employee-experience management market — the lane Vega’s EX focus targets — is ~$6.4B growing to ~$11.7B by 2030 at 9.7% CAGR (Grand View). T2 The third, the AI-moderated qualitative-research slice that Vega most precisely occupies, has no tier-1 standalone market figure at all — it is too new; vendor estimates run $1.3–2.8B with a 2× spread that itself signals how unsettled the number is. T3 An honest deck cannot put a precise multi-billion-dollar TAM on Vega’s actual slice; anyone who does is guessing.

Segment dynamics — where the money is, and where the white space is

The most important structural fact for this investment: the AI-research funding wave has crowded customer research, not employee research. Listen Labs, Outset, GetWhy, and Strella — the funded leaders — all aim primarily at customer / UX / market research, where the buyers (product, marketing, CX teams) and the budgets are largest and the comparison to Qualtrics/Medallia is cleanest. Vega is the only AI-interview player in this set positioned employee/organizational-first (cultural diagnostics, change readiness, post-merger integration). That is a genuine white space — and for EX work, the lack of a proprietary respondent panel matters far less, because you interview the client’s own employees. This is the single strongest structural argument in Vega’s favor.

The catch, developed below and in §8: that same white space is the most legally constrained segment in the market.

⚑ The regulatory point is sharper than it looks

Vega’s differentiator is decoding behavioural/emotional signals from interviews. When the subjects are employees in the EU, the EU AI Act’s workplace emotion-recognition prohibition (in force since Feb 2025) and works-council veto rights are not abstract compliance overhead — they bear on whether the core product can be sold, as-is, into the exact white-space lane (employee/EX research, EU) that is Vega’s best structural argument. This deserves a direct, specific question to the founders about how Vega is designed and positioned around Article 5.

The incumbents — the squeeze from below

Vega’s pitch (“surveys give scores, not meaning”) aims at the employee-experience giants, all verified at scale and all now adding conversational AI to install bases they already own: Qualtrics (taken private at $12.5B), Medallia ($6.4B), Culture Amp (~$1.5B), Microsoft Viva Glint (+ Copilot), and Workday Peakon. T2 They cannot match Vega’s conversational depth today — but they own the distribution, the data, and the customer relationship, and they are moving toward exactly the gap Vega occupies.

◆ The market read this lands on

This is a genuinely real, now-legitimized $150B+ category — but Vega is attacking it via a method that is itself the strategic problem. The category is commoditizing from above (foundation models absorbing the interview layer) and from below (funded incumbents already 18 months and $50–100M ahead, moving up into Vega’s lane). An unfunded, undifferentiated 2025 entrant sits on the wrong side of every moat that matters — and its one piece of white space, employee/EX research, is also the most legally constrained. The honest risk-adjusted market read is cautious, absent evidence of a defensible asset the funded comps lack. That asset, if it exists, is not in the public record — which is why the founder meeting is the whole game.

Competitive Landscape

Strategic read

Outset’s Series-B repositioning — from “AI interview tool” to “AI-native CX management platform,” explicitly aimed at Qualtrics and Medallia — is the most strategically telling move in the cohort. It signals where the category is heading: away from point tools and toward platforms that take on the incumbents directly — a larger, more defensible game that requires exactly the capital and enterprise muscle Vega lacks. And the panel-aggregation choice (renting reach from 25+ providers rather than owning it) reframes Vega’s missing panel as a category norm, not a unique deficiency — but it also means the durable moats here are turning out to be platform breadth and enterprise distribution, the two axes on which an unfunded entrant is most disadvantaged. For Vega the signal is double-edged: Outset is not chasing employee/org research, so the lane stays open — but its trajectory shows the category rewarding scale, which is the opposite of Vega’s position.

⚑ Verify the “AI-moderated” claim — it applies to GetWhy and to Vega

GetWhy’s independently verified capability (2024, CMO on record) is AI-analysis of unmoderated video — no live moderator in the loop. Live adaptive AI-moderation is a 2026 claim, not independently benchmarked. The lesson generalizes: “AI moderates the interview” is the category’s headline claim and the least independently verified — including Vega’s, which is self-reported only. Underwrite the verified layer (AI-accelerated analysis + recruitment + a staffed research agency); discount unbenchmarked “moderation leadership” claims, GetWhy’s and Vega’s alike.

Strategic read

GetWhy is the single most instructive comparator for valuing Vega’s model, because it is the same model — AI platform plus human research wrap plus full-service delivery — only fifteen years older, scaled, and funded. It proves the model can become a real business (~$84.5M raised, blue-chip logos, an independent ROI study). But it also shows what that business actually is: a research services company with proprietary AI, not a venture-scale software platform — which is precisely the “base case” read for Vega in §7. Two cautions transfer directly. First, the single-sponsor cap table (PeakSpan only) is a concentration signal worth noting in any private company, and Vega — seeking its first money — should be asked the same who-else-is-in question. Second, GetWhy’s most independently-verified capability is AI-analysis, not AI-moderation; the most-marketed claim is the least-proven. Apply that lens to Vega without flinching: its moderation-at-scale claim is self-reported only, and should be underwritten as a claim, not a capability, until demonstrated live.

Where it beats Vega

  • A real, staffed full-service research apparatus already operating.
Where Vega is distinct
  • GetWhy is consumer/brand-insight; Vega is org/EX — different buyer.
  • GetWhy’s advisory is research-craft; Vega claims higher-altitude strategy/I-O-psych advisory (pedigree, if real, is the only edge).

Vega’s human-advisory layer is its weakest place to plant a flag, not its strongest.

The methodology & the dataset — why the moat is genuinely uncopyable

ghSMART’s craft is the “Who” / A-Method (Smart & Street, Who: The A Method for Hiring, 2008), a structured four-interview selection process built on an outcome-driven Scorecard, descended from Topgrading, and delivered as 4-to-5-hour structured interviews per executive — often supplemented by interviews with the executive’s team and board. T1 That depth produces the real asset: a 30-year, structured, outcome-linked, longitudinal dataset. The numbers reconcile cleanly — the CEO Genome research dataset grew from 17,000 C-suite assessments (HBR 2017) to 27,000 C-suite executives including 2,000+ CEOs over ~15 years, while firm-wide leadership assessments total ~30,000. T1 The CEO Genome project was run by a 14-person research team including named academic economists (U-Chicago’s Steven Kaplan, Copenhagen’s Morten Sørensen), and produced HBR’s canonical “four behaviors” of successful CEOs. This is data on sitting C-suite executives and CEO candidates — career history linked to behavior linked to business results — that is nearly impossible to assemble from scratch and that no one else holds at this depth. It is the asset KKR is buying into; in a full-circle detail, KKR provided the data for Geoff Smart’s original PhD dissertation 30 years ago, which “facilitated the founding of ghSMART.” T1

The AI stack — the incumbent is shipping Vega’s claimed edge, with better governance

ghSMART’s “Leadership Intelligence Platform” is explicitly built on “the largest [proprietary database] of its kind, accumulated over 30 years”: Scorecard Builder (AI trained on thousands of sanitized ghSMART scorecards), askSMART (an internal research chatbot on anonymized data), a Next Gen Leadership Survey, and digitized SmartAssessments. T1 On top sits SHAPE-AI / SHAPEIndex (whitepapers 2025) — a framework to assess and develop “AI Velocity” in leaders, embedded into hiring scorecards. One detail cuts directly at Vega: ghSMART’s advisors are barred from public AI models, and askSMART runs only on anonymized internal data — a deliberate data-governance posture that stands in pointed contrast to the governance question hanging over Vega (the Jumpshot-advisor flag, §1). The incumbent is not only adding AI; it is adding AI with the data discipline Vega has not demonstrated.

And it is not the only incumbent moving

The broader advisory class is doing the same. Oliver Wyman — the alma mater Vega’s own advisors trade on — now fields a named AI practice, “Quotient — AI by Oliver Wyman,” and a Chief AI & Data Officer across Oliver Wyman/Marsh, on ~$3.4B revenue. T2 The strategy-consultancy class is bolting AI onto deep client relationships and balance sheets Vega cannot rival. The vise is real and two-sided: funded AI-research scale-players press from below, and elite advisory (ghSMART, Oliver Wyman) presses from above — both adding AI, both collapsing Vega’s wedge.

Where it out-classes Vega
  • 30 years, ~30k assessments, a real research bench — vs Vega’s essentially one-person-deep science anchor (Pierce) + a thin veneer.
  • Now AI-enabling, with KKR capital.
The two fair mitigants for Vega
  • Different lane: ghSMART does individual C-suite assessment; Vega does org-wide EX/culture/PMI — so it’s “the moat is occupied,” not head-to-head.
  • An AI-native cost structure could serve a scale/mid-market segment ghSMART’s 4–5-hour-interview economics can’t — if Vega has real software (the least-substantiated part of its team).

The Investment Logic · 07

Investment Logic & Scenarios

The case worked all the way through — bull, base, and bear — with what each scenario requires stated explicitly. This is the analytical core. It deliberately stops short of a buy/pass call, because that call depends on terms and on answers only the founders hold.

The bull thesis, stated at full strength

The strongest honest version: AI-moderated research is a real, now-legitimized wave, and Vega is early to the one lane the funded players have left open — employee/organizational research. It pairs that focus with the field’s most genuine science credential (Pierce). If Vega builds real software, lands an anchor enterprise or two, and accumulates a proprietary EX-interview dataset, it could become the category-definer for AI-native employee research the way Listen Labs became it for customer research — a category no incumbent currently owns. Senior operators + a real thesis + a hot category + first-money-in pricing is the shape of bet that occasionally returns many-fold. But every clause in that sentence is a conditional, and none is yet evidenced.

The bear thesis, stated at full strength

The heavier version on today’s evidence: Vega is the least-proven entrant in a field commoditizing from above (foundation models) and below (funded incumbents and Qualtrics/Culture Amp), with no funding, no customers, no owned panel or repository, no identifiable technical leadership, and a “moat” — behavioral-science advisory — that ghSMART out-classes by thirty years and is now AI-enabling with KKR money. Its one piece of white space, EU employee research, is also the most legally constrained segment in the market (Article 5(1)(f)). On this read, Vega is a high-touch services business with a thin AI veneer, vulnerable on every axis that determines durable value — “a feature in search of a moat.”

Three scenarios

▲ Bull

Vega becomes the “Listen Labs of employee research.” Owns real tech + a proprietary EX dataset; an anchor enterprise validates it; the EX lane proves defensible because incumbents are slow and the funded players stay in customer research.

Requires: owned IP, ≥1–2 referenceable customers, an accumulating data asset, a workable EU-compliance design, and capital to survive the enterprise sales cycle.

● Base

A credible boutique advisory/diagnostics firm. Wins specific high-touch EX / M&A-integration / AI-readiness engagements on relationships + the Pierce credential; profitable, single-digit-millions revenue; a real business but not venture-scale.

Implies: a modest return for early money if priced as a services business, not as a platform — the valuation-discipline question.

▼ Bear

A GPT-wrapper that never escapes pre-revenue. The advisory edge evaporates as ghSMART and incumbents add AI; the EX lane proves legally hard in the EU; no owned tech or data accumulates; funded competitors define the category. Capital impaired.

Triggered by: no real IP, no customers, evasive moat/data answers, a connector that doesn’t check out.

◆ What would have to be true for the bull case

Four things, none in the public record, all answerable in one founder meeting: (1) the founders own real, defensible technology — not a thin model wrapper; (2) at least one or two real, referenceable paying customers already exist; (3) there is a credible path to a moat the funded competitors lack — proprietary EX data, a wedge vertical, a compliance-by-design EU edge; and (4) the terms price early-stage, pre-traction risk honestly, and the connector’s track record checks out. If those four are true, the bull case is live and this is interesting. If they are not, the base case is the ceiling and the bear case is the risk. That is the decision — and it is a decision about evidence the investor can still go get, not about anything in this document.

The Investment Logic · 08

Risk Analysis

The risks ranked and developed — specific to this company and this evidence, not boilerplate. This is the protective read, and on today’s evidence it is the heavier side of the ledger.

1. No durable moat / commoditization — the existential risk

The interview engine is becoming table stakes (every funded rival has it; Vega’s is the least-verified). The model is not the moat — data, distribution, and workflow lock-in are, and Vega has none of them. The funded players are racing to build the only real moats (Listen’s living archive, proprietary panels, enterprise integrations); Vega is late and capital-starved into that race. The threat comes from both directions: foundation-model labs absorbing the thin-wrapper layer from above, and Qualtrics/Medallia/Culture Amp bolting conversational AI onto install bases from below.

2. The advisory “moat” is the most contested flank — not the strongest

This is the sharpest finding of the deep competitive work. Vega plants its flag on behavioral-science advisory. But ghSMART — 30 years, ~30,000 assessments, an uncopyable dataset — is that moat at full strength, and as of July 2025 is AI-enabling it with KKR capital. GetWhy also fields a staffed research/advisory apparatus at far greater scale. Vega’s advisory layer is essentially one credentialed academic (Pierce, genuinely excellent) plus a thin veneer. The place Vega claims to be strongest is the place a 30-year incumbent is best positioned to take.

3. Regulatory exposure on the one white-space lane (EU, EX)

Vega’s differentiator — decoding behavioural/emotional signals from interviews — collides with the EU AI Act’s Article 5(1)(f) ban on workplace emotion-recognition (in force since Feb 2, 2025) and with works-council veto rights when the subjects are EU employees. T1 The lane that is Vega’s best structural argument (employee research) is simultaneously its most legally constrained — a tension that needs a specific, designed answer, not a hand-wave.

4. Stage risk: unfunded, pre-traction, against funded competitors

Selling to leaders means long enterprise cycles, procurement, security review, and data-processing agreements. Rivals cross that with $18–96M and marquee logos behind them; an unfunded company must survive the 6–12-month cycle and win the “will you exist in two years?” vendor-risk conversation against a $500M-valued competitor. No prior institutional raise also means no one else has done this diligence — the investor is first, without a lead to lean on.

5. Team & governance risks

No identifiable full-time technical CTO for an AI product; titles inflated a notch across the roster; the deal-framing (executive assessment) differs from the public product (org research); the “multiple-exits investor” connector doesn’t map to the public team; and a data-privacy-scandal advisor (Baker/Jumpshot) sits on a roster for a company built on collecting intimate data. Individually minor; together they argue for verification before commitment.

⚑ The risk read, honestly

On the public evidence, the risk-adjusted read is cautious-to-negative — not because the idea is bad, but because Vega is on the wrong side of every moat that matters and its claimed edge is its most contested one. This read is reversible by facts the founders may hold (real IP, real customers, a real data strategy, a real EU-compliance design) and that this analysis cannot see. The weight of the risk is precisely why those founder answers carry so much — they are not detail, they are the decision.
