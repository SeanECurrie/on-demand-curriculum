The funding gap — does it cut the way it looks?

You said the funding gap concerns you. It’s a real factor — but the deeper research turned up a genuine counter-point worth holding alongside the worry, because a services/advisory business funds itself differently than a SaaS startup.

◆ The reframe: services revenue is its own runway

A pure-software startup runs on raised capital — cash burned ahead of revenue, with a finite runway (≈29% of startups die simply by running out of money). An advisory firm bills for cash on delivery: each engagement can be cash-positive, so a profitable services book has no finite runway as long as utilization holds — it’s funded by customers, not investors. The counterintuitive upshot: a profitable boutique can answer the enterprise buyer’s “will you still exist in two years?” better than a cash-burning, $500M-valued rival can. So the funding gap bites hardest in large, security-gated enterprise deals (long cycles, SOC 2, vendor-risk review) and least in mid-market HR/leadership deals where a profitable advisory shop reads as credible, not risky.

● The bear, kept honest — and the valuation fork

The flip side: services revenue caps growth velocity (you trade hours for cash while funded rivals buy GTM and R&D in parallel), and the data is sobering that services businesses rarely convert into high-multiple product companies. That creates the question that should set your price: the rich AI valuation multiples (~24× revenue in venture rounds) attach only to the software ARR — a “consultancy that owns a tool” is valued closer to a consultancy (low single-digit multiples). So the sharpest framing for your check: “am I pricing the software, or the consultancy?” The cap should reflect which business you’re actually funding.

◆ If it goes ahead — terms a first personal check usually wants (considerations, not advice)

Since you described this as personal funding, early, before outside money: at sub-$1M the market-standard instrument is a post-money capped SAFE (≈88% of pre-seed rounds). The mechanics a first check typically protects for: a valuation cap (non-negotiable — anchor against the ~$3–4M pre-seed band, flexed by their actual services revenue/profitability), an MFN clause (so a later, cheaper round doesn’t leave you worst-priced), pro-rata rights (to defend ownership through the priced round, since stacked SAFEs dilute), and basic information rights (there’s no board seat at this stage). Paper it properly even at small size — handshake angel deals tend to break at Series A. Flagged as considerations to raise with your own counsel, not as legal advice.
