Trajector Medical: the lawsuit, and what it means for you.

Trajector charged disabled veterans thousands of dollars for VA disability-claim help that federal law says must be free. It's now facing a nationwide class action, two ignored VA cease-and-desist letters, and a bill in Congress. Here's the whole picture — and the practical steps worth thinking about.

Class action filed Apr 2026 · C.D. Cal. Allegations unproven — Trajector denies them Not legal advice

The 90-second read

Trajector Medical is a for-profit company that helped veterans with VA disability claims and charged them for it — often four to five figures. The core of every complaint against it is one simple point: by law, that help is supposed to be free, from accredited people. Trajector was neither.

A nationwide class action now accuses Trajector of illegally charging disabled veterans thousands for claims help the law says is free. The allegations aren't proven yet — but the paper trail behind them is unusually strong.

Understand · 01

Who Trajector is

A large, fast-growing Florida company that turned "help a veteran document their conditions" into a business charging a percentage of the benefits the veteran won.

  • Founded 2014 as Vet Comp & Pen Medical Consulting; later rebranded to Trajector. Based in Gainesville, Florida. Founders: Jim Hill (Navy veteran) and Gina Uribe (a former VA medical examiner). confirmed1
  • Grew to 1,300+ employees by 2021 and even filed — then withdrew — SEC paperwork toward going public. This is a real company at scale, not a fly-by-night operation. confirmed1
  • The business model: Trajector's fee is contingent — roughly five times a veteran's monthly benefit increase if the claim succeeds. Reported bills ran $4,500 to $20,000+. confirmed12
  • Trajector describes itself as a "medical evidence" company — it says it helps veterans identify and document conditions, and insists it does not fill out or file VA claim forms. That distinction is the heart of its legal defense (see Section 8). its framing1
Understand · 02

Why the fees may be illegal

Two federal rules govern who can help with VA claims and whether they can charge. Understanding them is the whole case — and there's a twist that explains why this industry exists at all.

The transferable principle

Helping a veteran with a VA disability claim isn't an open market. Federal law restricts it to protect veterans from exactly this kind of fee-taking:

  • 38 U.S.C. § 5901 — accreditation. Only people accredited by the VA (attorneys, claims agents, or recognized service-organization reps) may assist with preparing or presenting a claim for pay. Trajector, the lawsuit says, was not accredited. legal risk2
  • 38 U.S.C. § 5905 — no fee for an initial claim. It is illegal to charge a veteran to prepare an initial disability claim. Accredited service organizations (VFW, American Legion, DAV, county veteran service officers) do this for free. legal risk6
⚑ The twist that explains everything

Charging for initial claims is illegal — but nearly two decades ago Congress stripped out the penalties for breaking that rule. So the conduct stayed illegal on paper while becoming effectively unenforceable. That enforcement gap is the vacuum a whole "cottage industry" of paid claims consultants grew into. This is the single most important thing to understand: the rule wasn't unclear — it just had no teeth. confirmed7

Trajector's counter (and why authorities reject it)

Trajector argues it only supplies medical evidence and doesn't "prepare" claims, so neither statute applies (see Section 8 for its full position). Independent authorities push back hard:

■ On the record against the defense

Former VA Inspector General Michael Missal said the approach "certainly violates the spirit and I would say it would violate the meaning of the law as well."1 The VA itself, in writing, told the company its materials "strongly suggest" it was unlawfully assisting with claims (see Section 5).

Honest read: This "evidence, not claims" distinction is the whole ballgame, and a court has not yet ruled on it. The plaintiffs think it's a fiction; Trajector is betting it's a real legal line. Nobody should treat the outcome as decided.

Understand · 03

The "CallBot" — the machine that turned this into a national story

More than the fees themselves, it's how Trajector allegedly collected them that drew investigators and Congress. Former employees described an automated system, internally nicknamed "the bloodhound."

  • CallBot repeatedly auto-dialed the VA Benefits Hotline using veterans' Social Security numbers and birthdates (collected in signed contracts) to detect when a rating or payment went up — reportedly across tens of thousands of veterans, with up to 20 concurrent calls running for days. alleged1
  • On detecting an increase, it automatically generated an invoice (5× the monthly increase) — regardless of whether Trajector actually caused the outcome — and collections began. alleged1
  • Tellingly: when the VA blocked Trajector's phone numbers, the company's collections revenue reportedly dropped — a sign of how central the automated dialing was. confirmed reporting1

Strengths & hurdles — an honest handicap

Unusually strong for plaintiffs: two written VA cease-and-desist letters and an NPR investigation with 11 former employees on record. Real hurdles: (1) Trajector's "medical evidence, not claim prep" defense is untested in court; (2) these contracts often bury arbitration / class-waiver clauses that defendants use to force disputes out of class actions into individual arbitration. Watch whether Trajector moves to compel arbitration — it's the most likely early battleground.

Understand · 05

The VA's two warnings — the paper trail

This isn't a surprise enforcement action. The VA told Trajector, in writing, twice — and then, per reporting, did nothing.

  • June 29, 2017
    VA Office of General Counsel sends a cease-and-desist letter (to the company then named Vet Comp & Pen). It says the company's materials "strongly suggest" it is "unlawfully assisting in the preparation, presentation and prosecution of claims" and charging for it — and orders it to "immediately cease." confirmed1
  • January 2022
    A 12-page follow-up letter says the practices violate federal law and threatens referral to "Federal and State law-enforcement authorities" if it doesn't stop. confirmed2
  • After 2022
    Trajector replies asserting it complies. Per NPR, the VA never followed up, and the company kept operating and billing. That inaction is now Exhibit A for why Congress says the law needs its teeth back. confirmed1
Understand · 06

Congress & regulators — the "claim shark" fight

A December 2025 NPR investigation put Trajector at the center of a national push to rein in companies charging veterans for free benefits — the so-called "claim sharks."

  • Dec 2025 — regulators put on notice. Roughly 40 members of Congress asked the VA, FTC, and CFPB to act. As of the reporting, Rep. Chris Pappas said they'd received no response. confirmed45
  • The GUARD Act. The bipartisan Governing Unaccredited Representatives Defrauding VA Benefits Act would reinstate the § 5905 penalties Congress stripped years ago — restoring fines for charging veterans for claims work. Currently stalled in the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs. confirmed8
  • The robo-dialer bill. A separate Pappas / Bacon bill would bar companies from using auto-dialers to call federal agencies — aimed directly at CallBot-style conduct. confirmed6
  • The counter-move. An industry-backed bill would instead legitimize paid consultants with a $12,500 fee cap. Note the tension: one track wants to ban the fees, the other to legalize-and-cap them. Worth watching which wins. confirmed6
For you · 07

What this means for Dad — steps to consider

If he used and paid Trajector, here's a practical checklist. To be clear: this is research, not legal advice — but these are the concrete, low-regret moves.

  1. Save everything. The contract, every invoice, proof of payment, and a log of any collection calls or messages. This is the evidence that matters whether he joins the class or disputes a bill. He likely never legally owed a fee for an initial claim — that's the whole premise of the suit.
  2. Read the contract for an arbitration / class-waiver clause. This one clause decides whether he can join the class action or would be pushed into private, individual arbitration. If he can't find it or can't tell, that's a specific question for a lawyer or VSO.
  3. Consider contacting the plaintiffs' firms. Aylstock, Witkin, Kreis & Overholtz and Bradley/Grombacher filed the case. If he thinks he's a class member, he can reach out to ask about eligibility and any coming class notice — usually at no upfront cost in these cases.
  4. If he's still being billed or in "collections," dispute it in writing. The debt's very legality is contested. Don't ignore it, but don't assume it's valid either — a written dispute preserves his rights.
  5. Revoke any standing authorization he gave Trajector to access his VA information (the SSN/birthdate authorization that powers CallBot), if it's still active.
  6. File complaints — they build the enforcement record. Regulators are under public pressure to act, and complaints are the raw material:
    • VA Office of General Counsel (unaccredited practice)  • FTC — reportfraud.ftc.gov  • CFPB (debt-collection)  • Florida Attorney General (50+ complaints already on file there).
  7. Get future help free, from an accredited source. VFW, American Legion, DAV, or his county Veteran Service Officer are VA-accredited and legally free — the exact service Trajector charged five figures for. Find a VSO at va.gov/ogc/apps/accreditation.
✓ The reassuring part

Nothing here jeopardizes Dad's benefits. The VA rating he was awarded is his — this dispute is strictly about the fee Trajector charged, not the disability award itself. The worst realistic case is a fee fight; the benefits stand.

Dig in · 08

Trajector's side — steelmanned

Fairness and honesty require stating their position at its strongest, not a strawman. Here it is.

  • "We provide medical evidence, not claims prep." Trajector's core argument is that it helps veterans document medical conditions and does not prepare, present, or file VA claims — so §§ 5901/5905 (which govern claims assistance) don't apply to it.1
  • "The complaint is without merit." Spokesperson Steve Zenofsky said the company "intends to defend itself vigorously," and points to a 4.6-star Google rating and an A+ BBB rating as evidence of genuine value to real veterans.3
  • "Veterans authorized it." Trajector says clients expressly authorized access to their VA information in signed contracts and can self-report increases — framing the data access as consented-to, not covert.1
⚑ Weighing it

The ratings and the "consent" point are real and shouldn't be dismissed — many veterans did get help. But two things cut against the defense: the VA told the company in writing, twice that its own materials looked unlawful, and a former VA Inspector General said the evidence/claims distinction violates "the meaning of the law." The defense is coherent, but it's swimming against a strong current. A court hasn't decided — and until it does, both of these are live.

Dig in · 09

What's still uncertain

Being precise about what we don't know is part of an honest brief. Open questions as of this writing (July 2026):

  • No ruling yet. The class action is months old. Nothing has been proven; the "alleged" tags throughout are load-bearing, not decoration.
  • Arbitration unknown. Whether Trajector's contracts force individual arbitration — which could gut the class action — hasn't surfaced publicly. It's the biggest single unknown for a would-be class member.
  • Regulators silent. As of the reporting, the VA/FTC/CFPB had not publicly responded to Congress. No confirmed formal FTC action against Trajector exists in the sources — despite the request for one.
  • Possible related suits. Given the counsel involved and the scale, additional or consolidated filings are plausible over time, but this brief documents the one confirmed nationwide case.
  • Legislation is a coin-flip. The GUARD Act is stalled and faces a competing industry bill. Which framework becomes law will shape whether this conduct is banned or capped-and-legalized.
