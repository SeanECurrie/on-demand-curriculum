Give Claude a job, not a chat.

Most people use AI like a very smart search box — and get search-box results. The unlock for real, repeating work isn't a better prompt trick. It's a different handoff: a mission, written once, that the AI can run without you. This page teaches that concept the way I teach it to real clients — with a real example that ran on real data.

The problem

Chat makes you the employee

Here's the same need — "I want to know when new businesses open in my part of town" — handled two ways:

THE CHAT WAY
"Hey, can you find new business licenses in Chicago this week?"
"Sure! Here are some I found…" (from wherever, formatted however)
— next Monday — "Hey, can you do that again? Like last time?"
"Of course! What area was it again?"
You show up every week, re-explain the job, and hope this week's answer matches last week's. The AI is bright; the arrangement is broken.
THE JOB WAY

You (or someone like me) write the job down once: exactly where the data lives, exactly what "new" means, what a normal week looks like, what it must never do, and what to do when something breaks.

Claude runs it on schedule. A clean email shows up Monday morning. You read it with coffee.

The knowledge lives in the mission, not in your memory. Anyone — including a brand-new Claude with no history — can run it correctly, forever.

The concept

The mission prompt has four parts

A mission prompt is not a longer chat message. It's a small employment contract, and every good one carries the same four things:

The goal

What done looks like, concretely. Not "keep me updated" — "every Monday at 8 AM, email me a digest of new business licenses in these ten zip codes."

The seeded facts

Things a human verified once so the AI never has to guess: where the data actually lives, which field is trustworthy, what normal volume looks like. This is where most of the value hides.

The guardrails

The nevers, written down: never email anyone else, never connect a new service silently, never deliver numbers you haven't sanity-checked. Trust comes from limits, not promises.

The fallback

What to do when something breaks: say so, plainly, and stop — never work around a wall silently. A system that admits confusion is a system you can leave alone.

A real one

The Monday-morning license digest

A real engagement from this summer: a Chicago client whose work depends on knowing when new businesses open in their neighborhoods. The old way was somebody remembering to go look. The mission below did exactly that in a live test-fire on real city data — it pulled a week from the City of Chicago's public records, sanity-checked it, and formatted it into one scannable email: 105 licenses issued, 13 applications found, verified, delivered.

Here's a condensed slice of the actual mission, color-coded to the four parts:

From the real mission prompt (condensed; client details removed)
Every Monday at 8:00 AM, build my Chicago business-license digest and email it to me.

Fetch from the City of Chicago "Business Licenses" dataset — public, free, no login:
data.cityofchicago.org/resource/r5kz-chrr.json. Expect roughly 70–120 licenses and
5–18 applications in a normal week (a real run measured 105 and 13).

THE FIELD TRAP: "newly issued" means the field date_issued. Do NOT use
license_start_date — the city future-dates and back-dates it (51 of 86 records
diverged in a tested week). The wrong field silently poisons the digest.

The window is 8 days on purpose — the city posts records 2–3 days late, and a
7-day window would drop them into a permanent blind spot. Do not "correct" this.

Sanity-check before delivering: if you see 0 results, or thousands — do NOT send.
Report plainly what you found and what you tried.

RULES: never send to any other address · never connect a new service without
saying what and why · never deliver numbers you haven't checked.

End every run with a plain-words note: the counts, whether they passed the check,
and whether the email went out. If you hit a wall, name it — never work around
it silently.
goal seeded fact guardrail fallback
Look closely at the field trap — that's the whole philosophy in one paragraph.

An AI asked casually for "new licenses" would grab the obvious-looking date field and be wrong — quietly, plausibly, every single week. A human caught that trap once, by checking the data by hand: 51 of 86 records had misleading dates. Then the human wrote the trap into the mission, and now no run can ever fall into it again — not even a brand-new AI with no memory of the discovery.

That's what AI enablement actually is. Not prompt tricks. A person who verifies reality once, then encodes what they learned so the machine stays right without them.

The setup, honestly

What it actually takes

No code, no engineer, no new software purchase. If you have a Claude account, the whole thing is a few minutes of clicking — which is exactly the point: the tool is usually already in the building. The missing piece is the handoff.

  1. Connect the delivery route

    In the Claude desktop app: Settings → Connectors → Gmail. Thirty seconds. This is how the result reaches an inbox instead of living in a chat window.

  2. Open Scheduled tasks

    Sidebar → Scheduled → New task. This is the difference between "asking every time" and "runs without you."

  3. Paste the mission

    The entire job — goal, seeded facts, guardrails, fallback — goes in as one block. The person never needs to read it, let alone write it. That's what they have me for.

  4. Verify the first run together

    The one step I never skip: the human confirms the first real output is right before anyone trusts the schedule. Automation earns trust with a supervised rep — same as any new hire.

Honest note: steps 1–3 are about three minutes of clicking. Step 4 is where an enablement person earns their keep — and it's also where most "AI rollouts" quietly fail, because nobody owns it.

The handoff craft

How you give this to a real person

The mission prompt is half the job. The other half is the handoff — because the person receiving this isn't technical, doesn't want to be, and shouldn't have to be. What the client actually received was a single friendly page, and its parts are a repeatable method:

A picture before any instructions

A three-box diagram — public records → your Claude → your inbox — with one line under it: "Your part in this diagram: nothing." People relax when they can see the shape of the thing.

A real sample, up front

The actual email from the test run, shown before asking them to do anything. Nobody sets up what they can't picture receiving.

One button, not a tutorial

The mission is a copy button. "Peek at what you're pasting (you never need to read it)" — curiosity welcomed, reading optional.

The weirdness, pre-explained

"Some entries say REDACTED FOR PRIVACY — that's the city masking home addresses, not a glitch." Every oddity they'll ever see, named before they see it. Confusion you prevent is trust you keep.

A change process in plain words

"Want it Fridays too? More zip codes? Just tell the task in plain words." The mission carries a section telling Claude how to accept upgrades cleanly — so the system grows without me standing next to it.

Scale that method from one busy person to a whole workforce and you have an AI adoption program: verify reality once, encode it into missions, hand each one over with craft, and own the supervised first rep. The tools change; that method doesn't.
