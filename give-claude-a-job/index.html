Give Claude a job, not a chat.

Most people use AI like a very smart search box — and get search-box results. This is a hands-on session on the other way: a mission, written once, that Claude runs without you. You'll read a real one, see what it delivers, set one up, and leave with a template you can run today.

Format: mini-course, 8 short lessonsYou need: a Claude account, no codeSource: a real engagement, test-fired on real data
LESSON 01

Why chat fails at real work

Same need — "I want to know when new businesses open in my part of town" — two handoffs. Click both tabs; the difference is the whole course:

"Hey, can you find new business licenses in Chicago this week?"
"Sure! Here are some I found…" (from wherever, formatted however)
— next Monday — "Can you do that again? Like last time?"
"Of course! What area was it again?"

The job is written down once: exactly where the data lives, what "new" means, what a normal week looks like, what it must never do, and what to do when something breaks.

Claude runs it on schedule. A clean email arrives Monday morning. You read it with coffee.

You are the employee: you show up weekly, re-explain the job, and hope this week matches last week.
The knowledge lives in the mission, not in your memory. A brand-new Claude with zero history runs it correctly, forever.
LESSON 02

The mission prompt: a small employment contract

A mission prompt is not a longer chat message. It's four things, every time:

  • The goal — what done looks like, concretely, with a schedule and a delivery address.
  • The seeded facts — things a human verified once so the AI never guesses: where the data actually lives, which field to trust, what normal volume looks like.
  • The guardrails — the nevers, written down. Trust comes from limits.
  • The fallback — when something breaks: say so plainly and stop. Never work around a wall silently.

Hold those four in mind — the next lesson has you find each one in a real mission.

LESSON 03

Read a real one

This is a condensed slice of an actual mission from a client engagement (details removed): a weekly digest of new Chicago business licenses, pulled from the city's public records. Click each part to see only its lines light up:

Click a part above. Each one exists because chat-mode fails without it — the goal stops drift, the facts stop guessing, the rails stop accidents, the fallback stops silent failure.
From the real mission (condensed; client details removed)
Every Monday at 8:00 AM, build my Chicago business-license digest and email it to me.

Fetch from the City of Chicago "Business Licenses" dataset — public, free, no login:
data.cityofchicago.org/resource/r5kz-chrr.json. Expect roughly 70–120 licenses and
5–18 applications in a normal week (a real run measured 105 and 13).

THE FIELD TRAP: "newly issued" means the field date_issued. Do NOT use
license_start_date — the city future-dates and back-dates it (51 of 86 records
diverged in a tested week). The wrong field silently poisons the digest.

The window is 8 days on purpose — the city posts records 2–3 days late, and a
7-day window would drop them into a permanent blind spot. Do not "correct" this.

Sanity-check before delivering: if you see 0 results, or thousands — do NOT send.
Report plainly what you found and what you tried.

RULES: never send to any other address · never connect a new service without
saying what and why · never deliver numbers you haven't checked.

End every run with a plain-words note: the counts, whether they passed the check,
and whether the email went out. If you hit a wall, name it — never work around
it silently.
Stop at the field trap — that's the whole philosophy in one paragraph.

An AI asked casually for "new licenses" would grab the obvious-looking date field and be wrong — quietly, plausibly, every week. A human caught that once, by checking the data by hand: 51 of 86 records had misleading dates. Then the human wrote the trap into the mission — and now no run can ever fall into it again, not even a brand-new AI with no memory of the discovery.

That is what AI enablement actually is: a person who verifies reality once, then encodes what they learned so the machine stays right without them.

LESSON 05

Set one up — the walkthrough

No code, no engineer, no purchase. If you have a Claude account, this is minutes of clicking — which is the point: the tool is usually already in the building; the missing piece is the handoff.

  1. Connect the delivery route

    Claude desktop app → Settings → Connectors → Gmail. Thirty seconds. This is how results reach an inbox instead of living in a chat window.

  2. Open Scheduled tasks

    Sidebar → Scheduled → New task. This is the difference between "asking every time" and "runs without you."

  3. Paste the mission

    The whole job — goal, seeded facts, guardrails, fallback — goes in as one block. Lesson 06 gives you a starter you can adapt.

  4. Verify the first run together

    The step never to skip: a human confirms the first real output before anyone trusts the schedule. Automation earns trust with a supervised rep — like any new hire.

Honest note: steps 1–3 are about three minutes of clicking. Step 4 is where an enablement person earns their keep — and where most "AI rollouts" quietly fail, because nobody owns it.

Your starter template

Leave with something you can run. Copy this, replace every [BRACKET], and paste it into a scheduled task — or just into a chat, to test the shape before you schedule it. The structure is the same one the real mission uses:

The template (edit the brackets, keep the bones) 
Set up a [weekly/daily] task: every [DAY] at [TIME], do the job below and
deliver it to [WHERE — an email address, a doc, this chat].

THE JOB
[One plain paragraph: what "done" looks like. Concrete enough that a
stranger could check it.]

SEEDED FACTS (a human verified these on [DATE] — trust them over guesses)
- The source: [where the data or input actually lives — a link if you have one]
- The trap: [the thing that looks right but is wrong, and what's right instead.
  If you don't know one yet, run the job by hand once — you'll find it.]
- Normal looks like: [expected size/shape of a result, so "wrong" is detectable]

RULES (non-negotiable)
- Never deliver anything to anyone except [WHERE].
- Never connect a new account or service without saying what and why first.
- Never deliver results you haven't checked against "normal looks like."

IF SOMETHING BREAKS
Say so plainly and stop. Report what you found and what you tried.
Never work around a wall silently.

End every run with a one-paragraph plain-words note: what happened, whether
the checks passed, and where the result went.

The hardest bracket is the trap — and that's on purpose. Finding it means doing the job by hand once, paying attention. That single supervised rep is what separates automation you trust from automation you babysit.

LESSON 07

When it breaks (it will, and that's fine)

A mission with a good fallback fails loudly and legibly. Here's the field guide the real engagement shipped with — the shape works for any mission:

What you seeWhat it means · what to do
The result mentions an approval clickNormal. Some connected services ask permission the first few times. Click it once; it stops asking.
"Counts were outside the normal range, so I didn't send"The guardrail working, not a failure. Open the task's chat, read what it found, decide together.
Nothing arrived at allOpen the task and read the last run's plain-words note — a good mission always leaves one. The answer is there.
The result looks slightly staleOften the source, not the system — many public datasets post days behind. A good mission's window accounts for it (the real one uses 8 days for this exact reason).
You want to change somethingTell the task in plain words ("add Fridays," "include the north side"). A good mission carries instructions for accepting upgrades cleanly.
LESSON 08

Handing it to a human

The mission is half the job. The other half is the handoff — the person receiving it isn't technical, doesn't want to be, and shouldn't have to be. What the client actually received was one friendly page, and its parts are a repeatable method:

A picture before any instructions

A three-box diagram — public records → your Claude → your inbox — captioned "Your part in this diagram: nothing." People relax when they can see the shape of the thing.

A real sample, up front

The actual email from the test run, shown before asking them to do anything (you saw it in Lesson 04). Nobody sets up what they can't picture receiving.

One button, not a tutorial

The mission is a copy button labeled "Peek at what you're pasting (you never need to read it)." Curiosity welcomed, reading optional.

The weirdness, pre-explained

"Some entries say REDACTED FOR PRIVACY — that's the city masking home addresses, not a glitch." Every oddity named before they meet it. Confusion you prevent is trust you keep.

A change process in plain words

"Want it Fridays too? Just tell the task." The mission itself carries instructions for accepting upgrades cleanly — the system grows without me standing next to it.

Scale that method from one busy person to a whole workforce and you have an AI adoption program: verify reality once, encode it into missions, hand each one over with craft, and own the supervised first rep. The tools will change; that method won't.
